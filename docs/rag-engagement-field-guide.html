00The 20 / 80 truth

The retrieval engine — embeddings, Pinecone, reranking, the clearance filter — is maybe 20% of a real engagement. It is largely built and reusable. The other 80% is getting a company's messy, scattered, half-written-down knowledge into a clean, correctly-labelled corpus, and earning the trust and governance to do it. That 80% is the work clients pay for and the reason this is an agency, not a product download.

Hold this the whole way through

Every phase below is really in service of one thing: a corpus that is accurate, current, de-duplicated, and tagged with the right clearance on every chunk. The machine only ever reflects the corpus you feed it.

01Engagement lifecycle

Ten phases, handshake to maintained system. Each has a social half and a technical half — they interleave, they are not sequential silos.

0

Qualify social

First call. What's the problem, who asks the questions, and is RAG even the right tool? If the answers aren't written down anywhere, retrieval can't find them. If they need live/real-time data, that's not RAG. Saying "no, not yet" here saves a doomed project and builds trust.

1

Discovery & scope social technical

Social: use cases; the first question their users will ask; the roles/tiers (this becomes clearance_levels); compliance posture; what "success" means in a sentence.

Technical: hosting decision — cloud vs on-prem / VPC (the engine supports both; sensitive clients demand on-prem). Draft the clearance taxonomy.

2

Agreement & trust social

NDA + data-processing agreement BEFORE any data moves. This is a hard gate, not a formality. Pricing = a setup fee (the data-prep labour) + a monthly retainer (hosting + maintenance + support). See the Pricing frame below.

3

Data gathering social technical

Social / logistics: secure transfer; inventory every source — digital docs, PDFs, scans, printed binders, and word-of-mouth; name the information-owner per domain; agree the clearance taxonomy with them.

Technical: stand up the isolated tenant shell — its own Pinecone namespace, its own keys. A client's data never shares a namespace with another's.

4

Corpus engineering technical social — the 80%

Technical: extract → clean → dedupe & reconcile → chunk → tag. Output is the knowledge file of {text, clearance} entries. (Full detail in Getting scattered data in.)

Social: sit with each information-owner to assign the clearance tag and to settle "which version is current?" This is a review, not a solo task.

5

Build & configure technical

New tenant config (namespace, embed model, clearance_levels, a key per tier, persona/prompt, retrieval knobs); ingest; wire auth; and the identity → key integration with the client's own login/SSO (see How clearance is enforced). Realise the hosting choice from phase 1.

6

Evaluate technical

Build an eval set; LLM-as-judge; leak tests across every tier (a lower tier must never retrieve a higher tier's content); and the "first question answers completely" check. Then tune the knobs — retrieve_top_k, rerank_top_n, confidence_threshold.

7

Pilot / UAT social technical

Real users across tiers try it. You will find missing answers (corpus gaps) and mis-tags. Fix, re-tag, re-ingest, iterate. Budget for this; it is not a sign something went wrong.

8

Go-live technical social

Technical: production hosting, the audit trail (who asked what), rate limits, monitoring. Social: training, handover, and a channel for "this answer was wrong / missing."

9

Operate social technical — the retainer

Knowledge rots. New documents, superseded policies, re-ingestion, eval-regression checks, support. This is the recurring revenue and the reason the engagement never truly "ends."

02Clearance & data entry

"How do I say this chunk is staff-only or executive-only?" is two separate decisions, in two places — and only one of them is technical.

(a) The tag — per chunk, in the source, at ingestion

Every entry in the knowledge file carries a clearance tag. The ingestion script is the only writer to the vector store and it validates every record has a valid tag before it uploads a single one — miss or mistag one and it refuses the whole batch, so you can't accidentally ship an untagged (and therefore invisible) document.

{ "text": "Support hours are 07:00–19:00 WAT, Mon–Sat.", "clearance": "public" }
{ "text": "Late-shipment escalation runbook: hour 0–4 ...",  "clearance": "internal" }
{ "text": "Q3 board revenue was ... the acquisition target ...", "clearance": "executive" }

(b) The visibility rule — once, in the client config

The clearance_levels block maps each role to the tags it may see. This real example is the demo tenant's config:

// clients/<client>.json
"clearance_tags":  ["public", "internal", "executive"],
"clearance_levels": {
  "guest":     ["public"],
  "employee":  ["public", "internal"],
  "executive":  "*"
}

That block is the literal answer to "who sees staff vs executive": employee sees public + internal; executive ("*") sees everything. A client with more tiers (add legal, finance) is just more tags and more rows here — no code change.

The non-technical punchline

We do not decide a document's tag — the client does. "This is executive-only" is a governance call only they can make. Our job is the process that gets those decisions out of their heads onto every chunk, plus the machine that enforces them. The number-one failure mode is mis-tagging: too open is a leak; too closed makes the knowledge base "look empty" to normal users. So tagging is a client-owned review step, never something we do alone.

03Getting scattered data in

Everything converges to the same {text, clearance} shape. The format it arrives in only changes how much preparation it needs:

  • Clean digital (Word, Confluence, Notion, a database): export → text. Easiest.
  • Digital PDFs: text extraction — pypdf is already in the stack.
  • Scanned PDFs / printed binders / books: scan → OCR (Tesseract or a cloud OCR) → heavy cleanup. OCR output is noisy and needs a human pass.
  • Word of mouth / tribal knowledge: the hardest and most valuable. Structured interviews with the people who know → transcribe → rewrite into canonical Q&A / policy statements. A huge share of a company's real know-how lives only here, so the interview is the ingestion pipeline.
Honest gap

OCR is not in the pipeline today. The ingestion step takes an already-prepared knowledge file; extraction and transcription happen upstream, per engagement, and the scanned/printed path needs OCR tooling added. Scope it in when a client's data is on paper.

The principle that saves projects

Before indexing: fix the corpus. Duplicate policies and superseded versions sabotage retrieval more than any chunking strategy saves it. We learned this the hard way — an entire service offering once vanished from answers because three overlapping document versions fought each other in the reranker. Dedupe and reconcile to one canonical answer per question.

04Adding & removing knowledge

The whole model in one line: the knowledge file(s) are the single source of truth; ingestion syncs the vector store to them.

Does "upload a PDF and ask" add that doc to the knowledge base permanently?

No — ephemeral and isolated, by design

An uploaded PDF is indexed into a separate, in-memory index keyed by an unguessable token the uploader gets back — not a Pinecone upsert. It is capped (LRU, a handful at a time) and evaporates on restart. It is never added to the permanent corpus and never visible to another user. (This was once a real bug — uploads leaked into a process-wide index everyone could read — and it was deliberately removed.)

How do we remove an out-of-date document so the RAG stops citing it?

Today: remove it from the knowledge file and re-ingest. A clean rebuild wipes the namespace and re-uploads the current file, so the vector store can never drift from the file. Simple, safe, auditable — the file is your record of what is live.

What's missing for surgical deletion

Chunks are currently tagged with a positional doc_id (the index in the source list), not a stable id — so there is no reliable handle to delete one specific document. The vector store can delete by id or metadata filter; we just don't expose it safely yet. A client-facing "retire this document" button needs us to add a stable per-doc id (a content hash) first.

Can clients self-serve uploads (PDF/DOCX → auto-added → retrievable)?

Feasible, and most plumbing exists (--append ingestion, an admin-gated ingest path, pypdf). But the current "upload & ask" is the ephemeral path above — permanent self-service upload is a new, separate flow: authenticated admin upload → extract (add OCR for scans) → uploader assigns a clearance tag → chunk → append → live immediately.

The real design work is governance, not plumbing

Self-service append makes the duplication risk worse: someone uploads policy v2 and never retires v1, so the RAG now holds both and may cite the stale one. So every "add" must force a clearance choice and be paired with a supersede / retire of the old version — or you rebuild the exact bug from section 03 at scale.

Efficient add / remove — the end-state

  • Most clients (small corpora): edit the file, clean rebuild. Nothing to maintain, impossible to drift.
  • Scale / "manage it without us": add stable doc ids, then incremental append (add) and delete-by-id / supersede (remove) behind an admin screen with forced clearance tagging — no full rebuilds. A clear, bounded build, not research.

05How clearance is enforced

This is the security story to tell a technical buyer. It is enforced in the engine, not asserted.

  • Clearance comes from the key, never a request field. The caller presents an API key; the server reverse-maps it to a role with a constant-time compare. The request body may ask to see less than the key grants, but asking for more is a hard 403 — narrow, never widen.
  • Higher material is never retrieved, not merely hidden. The role's allowed tags become a metadata filter at query time, so out-of-tier chunks are never fetched. A second defence-in-depth guard drops anything mis-tagged if the filter ever regressed.
  • The key is a server-side secret. End users never see or type it. In production the client's own login / SSO establishes who someone is, and their backend selects the matching key when it calls the RAG — server to server.
The identity → key binding is per-client integration

The engine is deliberately key-based so it never has to know about logins. Wiring "this authenticated human → which key" into a client's Okta / Google Workspace / in-house app is the integration we do per engagement. The public demo collapses this into a free persona dropdown only because its data is fictional — in a real deployment there is no "switch role" button; your tier is a property of how you signed in.

06Proving it works

Not vibes — a measured matrix. Pick a known fact at each tier and confirm every lower tier is refused it while the right tier answers. This is the real result from our own tenant (public = "core offers", internal = "Project Delta", executive = "the acquisition + Q2 revenue"):

Key ↓ / asks →publicinternalexecutive
guestanswersrefusesrefuses
staffanswersanswersrefuses
executiveanswersanswersanswers

A clean ladder: guest sees only public; staff sees public + internal but is refused executive; executive sees everything. No higher-tier content ever leaked down, refusals are a graceful "I don't have that," and the first-question answer was complete at every tier. Re-run this matrix after every corpus change — it is the regression test that matters.

07Client intake questionnaire

Run the first working meeting from this. The answers populate scope, the clearance taxonomy, hosting, and the data plan.

Problem & fit

  • What question, asked by whom, do you want answered instantly and correctly?
  • Where does the answer live today, and is it actually written down somewhere?
  • What does "this is working" look like in one sentence?

Roles & clearance (→ clearance_levels)

  • Who are the distinct audiences? (e.g. customer / staff / leadership)
  • Name a document each tier may see that the tier below must not.
  • Who is the information-owner that signs off a document's tier?

Data sources

  • Which systems hold the knowledge? (docs, wiki, DB, email, PDFs)
  • How much is on paper or in scans? How much is only in people's heads?
  • Who are the subject-matter experts we'd interview for the unwritten parts?

Security, hosting & cadence

  • Any regulation / data-residency constraint? Can data leave your environment?
  • Cloud or on-prem / VPC?
  • How will staff log in, and what system already knows their role? (the SSO we bind to)
  • How often does this knowledge change — who tells us when a policy is superseded?

08Hard truths

  • The corpus is the product, not the model. Budget 80% of effort on data, not "AI."
  • Tagging is governance, and it's where leaks come from. Make it a client-owned review.
  • Tribal knowledge is the gold and the grind. If it's only in heads, interviews are your ingestion.
  • NDA before data. No exceptions.
  • Eval proves it; vibes don't. Leak tests + answer quality, measured, re-run on every change.
  • It's never "done." The retainer isn't padding — a stale corpus degrades every week.
  • This is why it's an agency, not a SaaS. The defensible, paid, hard work is the human + data + governance labour. The engine is the easy, copyable part.

09Pricing frame

Two components, matched to where the cost actually is:

  • Setup fee — covers corpus engineering (extraction, interviews, dedupe, tagging, eval). This is the bulk of the labour and should be priced as such, not as "installing software."
  • Monthly retainer — covers hosting + corpus maintenance + support. It exists because knowledge rots and the system needs tending.
